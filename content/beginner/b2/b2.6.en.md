::: step hook
Lisa is opening a café in Lisbon and asks for a name:

```prompt
Come up with 5 names for a café. I'd like something warm and unusual.
```

```claude
1. Coffee Time
2. The Cosy Cup
3. Coffee Corner
4. Aroma
5. Morning
```

That's not at all what Lisa means by “warm and unusual”. How can she explain what she has in mind?
:::

::: step explain
Style is hard to put into words. “Unusual” means different things to Lisa and to Claude.

But it's easy to *show*. Like at the hairdresser's: it's easier to show a photo of the haircut than to explain “shorter, but not too short”.

The people who make Claude call examples one of the most reliable ways to set the format and tone of an answer.
:::

::: exercise compare_answers
id: "b2.6.e1"
type: "compare_answers"
variant: "why_better"
question: "Lisa added two examples. Why is the second answer closer to what she wanted?"
cards:
  - id: "A"
    label: "No examples"
    prompt: "Come up with 5 names for a café. I'd like something warm and unusual."
    answer: |
      1. Coffee Time
      2. The Cosy Cup
      3. Coffee Corner
      4. Aroma
      5. Morning
  - id: "B"
    label: "With examples"
    prompt: "Come up with 5 names for a small café in Lisbon's Alfama district. I like names such as “Grandma's Windowsill” and “Sometime on a Thursday”: they have a little story and a smile in them. For each name, add one line on why it works."
    answer: |
      1. “The Neighbour Upstairs” — like dropping in on a friend for coffee.
      2. “Before It Gets Cold” — about not rushing here.
      3. “The Warm Stairwell” — the cosiness of old Alfama houses.
      4. “Five More Minutes” — about a morning you don't want to let go of.
      5. “A Cup by the Window” — a simple picture you see straight away.
options:
  - text: "The examples showed the style: a little story and a smile, not generic words about coffee"
    correct: true
    why: "Yes. Claude had its own take on “warm and unusual”. Two examples showed what Lisa meant: short phrases that tell a story. And all the new names are in that spirit."
  - text: "Claude just copied Lisa's examples"
    correct: false
    why: "None of the names repeats the examples. Claude took the style from them, not the words. That's exactly how examples should work."
  - text: "The second prompt has more words, so the answer is better"
    correct: false
    why: "Word count on its own doesn't help. What helped was two precise samples and an explanation of what she likes about them."
  - text: "It's only because the second prompt mentions Alfama"
    correct: false
    why: "The neighbourhood added local colour (“The Warm Stairwell”), but the “little story” style came from the examples. Without them, it would have been “Aroma” and “Morning” again."
hint: "Compare the names in the second answer with Lisa's examples. What do they have in common, even though the words are different?"
:::

::: step explain
What makes a good example:

- *similar* to what you need: an example name for names, an example post for posts;
- *different* from each other: if both examples are about a grandma, Claude will decide a grandma is required;
- *separate* from the request: “Here are some examples I like:”.

You can start with 1–2 examples. For the best results, Anthropic recommends 3–5.
:::

::: exercise single_choice
id: "b2.6.e2"
type: "single_choice"
question: "Jake wants Claude to come up with Instagram captions for photos of his dog. Which pair of examples is better?"
options:
  - text: "“Guarding the sofa from dangerous cushions” and “Monday? Never heard of it”"
    correct: true
    why: "The examples are different: one's about the home, the other's about a mood. But the style is the same: a short joke in the dog's voice. Claude will pick up the style, not the topic."
  - text: "“Guarding the sofa from cushions” and “Guarding the bed from blankets”"
    correct: false
    why: "The examples are too similar. Claude may decide every caption has to start with “Guarding…” and give you ten identical jokes."
  - text: "“My dog is very cute” and “Lovely photo”"
    correct: false
    why: "These examples don't have the style Jake wants. Claude will copy the dull tone too, because it follows the examples."
  - text: "Better without examples, Claude will figure it out"
    correct: false
    why: "Sometimes it will, but everyone's idea of “funny” is different. A couple of examples is the quickest way to show what kind of humour you like."
hint: "Good examples share a style, but not a topic or the same words."
:::

::: exercise fill_gap
id: "b2.6.e3"
type: "fill_gap"
question: "Finish the prompt for Marta, who writes reminders for clients of her salon in Prague."
text: "Write 3 text message reminders for manicure appointments. Here's ___ I like: “Anna, we look forward to seeing you tomorrow at 3 pm. If your plans have changed, just reply to this message.” Write them ___ and keep each one ___."
gaps:
  - options:
      - text: "an example of the tone"
        correct: true
        why: "Marta calls it an example outright. Claude will understand it's a sample of the style, not finished text to copy."
      - text: "a text"
        correct: false
        why: "Then Claude might decide this text should just be repeated or edited. The word “example” is more precise."
      - text: "an order"
        correct: false
        why: "It's not an order, it's a sample. What matters is that Claude understands it should take the style."
  - options:
      - text: "in the same style, but for different situations: an appointment tomorrow, one next week, a rescheduled one"
        correct: true
        why: "This asks Claude to keep the style of the example and change the content. That way the three messages will be different, not copies of one."
      - text: "exactly the same way"
        correct: false
        why: "Then Claude might give you three almost identical messages with different names. Better to say what to keep (the style) and what to change (the situation)."
      - text: "not like the example"
        correct: false
        why: "This contradicts the example itself, and on top of that it's a ban, not a description."
  - options:
      - text: "under 160 characters, so it fits in a single text"
        correct: true
        why: "A specific length and a reason: format and the “why” from earlier lessons."
      - text: "not too long"
        correct: false
        why: "This is a ban with no measure. How many characters? For texts, that matters."
      - text: "however you like"
        correct: false
        why: "For text messages, length is critical. If you don't set it, the text may get split into two messages."
hint: "Let Claude know it's a sample of the style, not text to copy. And remember how to set the length."
:::

    correct: true
    why: "The role sets the angle, the examples show the style, the context explains what kind of café it is. All the techniques together."
  - text: "Just a role: “You're a genius copywriter”, no examples needed"
    correct: false
    why: "A role alone won't show Lisa's taste. “Genius” gives no focus, and without examples the style has to be guessed again."
  - text: "10 identical examples with no role and no context"
    correct: false
    why: "Lots of identical examples lead to copies. And without context, Claude doesn't know what café the names are for."
hint: "Role, context and examples don't compete with each other. Look for the option where they work together."

::: step explain
Now it's your turn. Ask Claude to come up with 5 names for a café and show 2 examples of a style you like.

You can make up the examples or use names of places you know. The key is that they're different from each other. Navyk will send your prompt for feedback.
:::

  criteria: ["R1", "R2", "R4"]

::: step summary
**Can't describe a style? Show it with 1–2 different examples.**

Say that they're samples, and what you like about them. Claude will take the style, not the words.
