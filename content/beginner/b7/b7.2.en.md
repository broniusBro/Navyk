::: step hook
Anna from Lyon asks Claude about her insurance for a trip to Prague.

```prompt
Does my insurance cover dental treatment abroad?
```

```claude
Yes, most travel insurance policies cover emergency dental treatment up to a certain amount.
```

Sounds reassuring. But Claude never saw her policy. It didn't know the answer and answered anyway. Could that have been avoided?
:::

::: step explain
Claude really wants to help, so it sometimes fills gaps with guesses. One simple phrase helps: *permission to say “I don't know”*.

It's like a new colleague: if the manager says outright “not sure? Ask me”, they'll honestly check instead of guessing.

Anthropic says this simple technique noticeably reduces the number of made-up answers.
:::

::: exercise compare_answers
id: "b7.2.e1"
type: "compare_answers"
variant: "why_better"
question: "Anna added one sentence to her prompt. Why is the second answer more useful?"
cards:
  - id: "A"
    label: "Before"
    prompt: "Does my insurance cover dental treatment abroad?"
    answer: |
      Yes, most travel insurance policies cover emergency dental treatment up to a certain amount.
  - id: "B"
    label: "After"
    prompt: "Does my travel insurance cover dental treatment in the Czech Republic? I'm not attaching the policy terms yet. If you can't answer precisely from this, say so directly and tell me what to check in my policy."
    answer: |
      I can't answer that precisely: it depends entirely on the terms of your particular policy.
      Check these points in it:
      1. Whether there's a section on dental care or “acute toothache”.
      2. The cover limit for that kind of treatment.
      3. Whether you need to call the insurer before going to a dentist.
      If you send me the policy text, I'll go through it with you.
options:
  - text: "Claude honestly said it didn't know the answer and showed where to find it"
    correct: true
    why: "Yes. Instead of a comforting guess, Anna got an honest “I can't say for sure” and a plan for checking. Without it, she might have travelled with false confidence and ended up with a dentist's bill."
  - text: "The second answer is better because it's longer"
    correct: false
    why: "Length has nothing to do with it. The first answer is shorter, but its confidence is what makes it dangerous. The second is useful because it honestly separates what's known from what isn't."
  - text: "The first answer is better: it says “yes” straight away and puts her mind at rest"
    correct: false
    why: "It puts her mind at rest with nothing to back it up: Claude never saw the policy. If that “yes” turns out to be wrong, it's Anna and her wallet that pay."
hint: "Compare them: in which answer does Claude pretend to know something it can't know?"
:::

::: step explain
Handy phrases you can add to almost any factual prompt:

```prompt
If you're not sure or don't have enough information, say so.
```

```prompt
Separate what you know for certain from what you're unsure about.
```

```prompt
Give a source for each claim. If there's no source, remove the claim.
```

For a file, add: “First pull out word-for-word quotes from the document, then draw your conclusion.” You've already seen this in the lesson on documents.
:::

        correct: false
        why: "Quite the opposite: this bans honesty. If Claude doesn't know, it'll still answer confidently, and a mistake could cost you the ticket price."

        correct: false

        correct: true

      - text: "add an emoji"

        why: "An emoji won't help you check a rule. What you need is support: where the claim came from."

::: step explain
In some areas the cost of a mistake is especially high: health, money, documents. Here Claude helps you *prepare*: understand the terms, put together questions, not miss anything.

The decision is made by a professional. For health, that's a doctor. No medication doses or diagnoses from a chat: that's like choosing medicine on the advice of a stranger on the bus.
:::

::: exercise single_choice
id: "b7.2.e3"
type: "single_choice"
question: "Marta from Kraków has had a sore throat and a temperature for three days. Which prompt to Claude is the most sensible?"
options:
  - text: "“Help me prepare for a visit to my GP: what questions to ask and what to tell them about my symptoms. If any of the symptoms mean I should get help urgently, tell me directly.”"
    correct: true
    why: "Claude helps her prepare, and the decision stays with the doctor. And Marta gave it permission up front to be direct about warning signs. That's how AI is both useful and safe."
  - text: "“Which antibiotic should I take, and what dose?”"
    correct: false
    why: "Only a doctor prescribes medication and doses, after examining you. A mistake here costs your health, and Claude can't see Marta, doesn't know her medical history and can get it wrong."
  - text: "“Diagnose me from my symptoms, I don't want to see a doctor”"
    correct: false
    why: "A diagnosis by message is unreliable even from a doctor. Claude can explain what those symptoms can point to, but it can't replace an examination."
  - text: "“Tell me it's just a cold so I can stop worrying”"
    correct: false
    why: "This directly asks for a convenient answer instead of an honest one. Claude can't know whether it's a cold or not: that's for a doctor to determine."
hint: "Which prompt leaves the decision to the doctor and gives Claude the role of helper?"
:::

  - text: "After this phrase, Claude never makes mistakes"

::: step summary
**Let Claude say “I don't know”: “If you're not sure or don't have enough information, say so.”**

Add a request for a source. And for health, money and documents, Claude helps you prepare, while the decision is up to a professional.
