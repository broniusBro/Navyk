::: step hook
Emma, a student in Galway, is writing a term paper and asks Claude to find sources.

```prompt
Give me 3 academic articles on how social media affects students' sleep. Include authors, year and journal name.
```

```claude
1. Kowalczyk, T., & Lee, D. (2019). “Digital habits and sleep quality in university students”. Journal of Applied Psychology, 14(2).
2. …
```

The list looks solid. But the journal has no such article. How did that happen?
:::

::: step explain
This is a *hallucination*: Claude confidently writes something that isn't real.

It's like a passer-by who won't admit they don't know the way and cheerfully points “that way, round the corner”. They're not lying to trick you, they just really want to help.

Anthropic itself calls these hallucinations and openly warns that all modern AI models have them.
:::

::: exercise single_choice
id: "b7.1.e1"
type: "single_choice"
question: "What is a Claude hallucination?"
options:
  - text: "An answer that sounds confident and plausible but contains made-up facts"
    correct: true
    why: "Yes. The real danger is that you can't hear a fabrication in the tone: it's formatted just as neatly as the truth. Like the article in Emma's example: author, year, journal, everything is there except the article itself."
  - text: "An answer with typos and grammar mistakes"
    correct: false
    why: "Typos are easy to spot and do little harm. A hallucination is sneakier: the text can be perfectly written while the facts in it are invented."
  - text: "When Claude refuses to answer a question"
    correct: false
    why: "A refusal or “I don't know” is actually an honest answer. A hallucination is the opposite: Claude doesn't know, but answers confidently anyway."
  - text: "A glitch that only happens on the free plan"
    correct: false
    why: "The plan has nothing to do with it. Every modern AI model can hallucinate, so everyone needs the habit of checking."
hint: "Think of the passer-by cheerfully giving directions they don't actually know."
:::

::: step explain
Where made-up details hide most often:

- exact figures, dates and amounts;
- quotes and “as a famous person once said”;
- links, titles of articles and books, numbers of laws;
- recent news and narrow local topics.

A confident tone and neat formatting prove nothing. The more precise and important a detail, the more carefully you should check it.
:::

::: exercise multi_choice
id: "b7.1.e2"
type: "multi_choice"
question: "Ben from London asked Claude how to claim back VAT on a laptop he's buying on holiday in France. Which parts of the answer should he double-check first? Select all."
options:
  - text: "The minimum purchase amount for a refund, given to the euro"
    correct: true
    why: "An exact amount is a classic place for a mistake. And the rules change. Check it against the official tax authority website or with the shop."
  - text: "A link to the “official page” with the rules"
    correct: true
    why: "Claude can stitch links together from similar-looking pieces, and they lead nowhere. Open the link and make sure the page exists and says the same thing."
  - text: "The deadline for getting the form stamped at the border"
    correct: true
    why: "Deadlines are money: miss one and there's no refund. Check details like this against the original source."
  - text: "Advice to keep the receipt and the packaging until the border"
    correct: false
    why: "That's general common sense: even if it isn't required, it does no harm. Check exact figures, deadlines and links first."
  - text: "An offer to lay out the required documents as a checklist"
    correct: false
    why: "That's the shape of the answer, not a fact. There's nothing to check here: a handy checklist just helps you keep track."
hint: "Look for exact figures, deadlines and links: things that can be made up and that cost money if they're wrong."
:::

::: step explain
What do you do with a suspicious answer? Ask what it's based on:

```prompt
What is this answer based on? Give a source for each claim. If there's no source or you're not sure, say so.
```

If Claude searched the web, it will show links. Open them: Anthropic recommends reading the sites themselves, as they often contain important details that a summary leaves out.
:::

::: exercise find_error
id: "b7.1.e3"
type: "find_error"
question: "This prompt is practically asking for made-up content. Tap every fragment that pushes Claude towards a hallucination."
prompt: "Give me 5 quotes from famous people about saving money, each with the book and page number. Answer confidently, no “maybe” or “I'm not sure”."
segments:
  - text: "Give me 5 quotes from famous people about saving money, "
    weak: false
    why: "The request itself is fine and clear. Claude may know some quotes, but they're worth checking: plenty of sayings get wrongly credited to “famous people”."
  - text: "each with the book and page number. "
    weak: true
    why: "A page number is exactly the kind of precise detail that's easy to invent. If Claude doesn't remember it for certain, insisting on it nudges Claude to fill the gap. Better: “give the source if you know it for sure”."
  - text: "Answer confidently, no “maybe” or “I'm not sure”."
    weak: true
    why: "This outright bans honesty. You take away Claude's right to say “I don't know”, so a doubtful quote arrives with the same confidence as a verified one."
hint: "Find the places where the prompt demands precision at any cost or forbids doubt."
:::

::: exercise single_choice
id: "b7.1.e4"
type: "single_choice"
question: "Claude writes: “A parcel from the US to the EU is free of customs duty if its value doesn't exceed a certain amount” and gives an exact figure. You're about to order expensive headphones. What's the best next message?"
options:
  - text: "“Where does that figure come from? Give me a source. If you're not sure it's up to date, say so.”"
    correct: true
    why: "You're asking for support and allowing Claude to admit doubt. Then open the source and check the figure on the official customs website: duty rules change."
  - text: "“Thanks, got it!”"
    correct: false
    why: "You're taking an exact figure on trust, and your money depends on it. If it's outdated or made up, you'll pay duty on top."
  - text: "“Are you sure? Be honest!!!”"
    correct: false
    why: "Claude may simply say “yes”. “Are you sure?” gives it no way to check itself. Asking for a source works better: you can check that."
  - text: "“Say that again, but shorter”"
    correct: false
    why: "Shorter doesn't mean more accurate. The same figure in a shorter answer is still unchecked."
hint: "Which message gives you something you can open and check yourself?"
:::

::: exercise order_steps
id: "b7.1.e5"
type: "order_steps"
question: "Claude's answer matters: money or deadlines depend on it. Put the checking steps in order."
shuffle: true
items:
  - text: "Spot the exact figures, dates, links and quotes in the answer"
    why: "First find what actually needs checking. These are exactly the places where made-up details tend to hide."
  - text: "Ask Claude to give a source for each claim"
    why: "The answer becomes verifiable: every fact gets an “address”. This is exactly how Anthropic recommends reducing hallucinations."
  - text: "Open the sources and check the answer against them"
    why: "The key step. A link may not exist or may say something else, and the original often has important details."
  - text: "Rely on what's confirmed, and drop the rest or check it with an expert"
    why: "Not confirmed? Don't use it. For money, health and documents, the official source or an expert has the final say."
hint: "First find what to check, then ask for support, then check it yourself."
:::

::: step summary
**A confident tone isn't the same as the truth. Check exact figures, dates, quotes and links.**

Ask: “What is this answer based on? Give a source for each claim.” Then open the source yourself.
