::: step hook
For three weeks, Sam from Leeds has kept one single chat with Claude: first choosing a laptop, then writing to the landlord, then preparing for a job interview.

```prompt
Give me a short list of questions to ask the employer.
```

```claude
Here are some questions. And since you're choosing a laptop for work, ask whether the company provides equipment…
```

Claude is mixing up old topics with the new one. Why does that happen?
:::

::: step explain
When Claude answers, it takes into account everything in the chat. That's its *context*, or “working memory” for the conversation.

Picture a desk. With one folder on it, you find what you need straight away. With three weeks of papers piled up, it's easy to grab the wrong one. Anthropic says accuracy drops as the amount of text grows.
:::

  - text: "Claude takes everything in the chat into account, and old topics get in the way of the new task"
    correct: true
    why: "Yes. Like a desk buried in papers: what you need is there, but there's a lot of clutter around it. That's how the laptop ended up in Sam's interview questions."
  - text: "Claude gets tired from a long conversation, like a person"
    correct: false
    why: "Claude doesn't get tired. It's about the amount of text it holds in its “working memory”: the more there is and the more mixed it is, the easier it is to blend topics."
  - text: "Claude gets offended if you talk to it for too long"
    correct: false
    why: "Claude doesn't have feelings to hurt. It's simply that extra context makes it harder to focus on the current question."
  - text: "Long chats get deleted, so Claude forgets everything"
    correct: false
    why: "Quite the opposite: the problem is that it remembers too much, and much of it off-topic. The chat itself doesn't disappear."
hint: "Think of the desk with three weeks of papers on it."
:::

::: step explain
A long chat has a second cost too: it uses up more of your *limit*. Your limit is how much you can use Claude in a given period, like the minutes in a mobile phone plan.

Anthropic advises it directly: if you're approaching your limit in a long chat, start a new conversation.
:::

  - left: "You're done with the email and now need a dinner recipe"
    right: "Open a new chat"
    why: "New task, new chat. The email won't help with the recipe; it'll just take up “working memory” and your limit."
  - left: "You're correcting Claude on the same thing for the third time, and it keeps getting it wrong"
    right: "Start a new chat with a prompt that includes everything you've figured out"
    why: "The chat is full of failed attempts, and they keep throwing Claude off. Anthropic advises: after two failed corrections, it's better to start fresh with a more precise prompt."
  - left: "The chat has grown huge, but it's the same topic and the work isn't finished"
    right: "Ask for a short summary and continue in a new chat"
    why: "That way you keep the essentials and clear away the rest. The new chat starts with a clean desk, but with the papers you need."
hint: "Ask yourself: is it the same task or a new one? And is the old context helping or getting in the way?"
:::

::: step explain
How do you move to a new chat without losing anything? Ask Claude to put together a summary:

```prompt
Write a short summary of our conversation: what we decided, what information matters and what's left to do. I'll carry it over to a new chat.
```

Copy the summary, open a new chat, paste it in and add your next task straight away.
:::

::: step summary
**New task, new chat. A long chat mixes up topics and eats into your limit.**

If it's the same topic but the chat has grown huge, ask for a short summary and carry it over to a new chat. After two failed corrections, start fresh with a more precise prompt.
