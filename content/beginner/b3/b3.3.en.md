::: step hook
Emma rents a flat in Dublin and wants to move out before the lease ends. She uploaded her lease and asked:

```prompt
[attached file: lease_agreement.pdf]
Can I move out early?
```

```claude
Yes, a tenant can usually end the lease early by giving the landlord one month's notice. The deposit is generally returned.
```

“Usually”, “generally”... Is that about her lease or about leases in general?
:::

::: step explain
Claude sometimes fills gaps with general knowledge and still sounds confident. This is called a *hallucination*: like a friend retelling a film they didn't finish and making up the ending.

With a document, there's a safeguard: ask Claude to *find quotes first*, meaning word-for-word passages from the text, and only then answer.
:::

::: exercise compare_answers
id: "b3.3.e1"
type: "compare_answers"
variant: "why_better"
question: "Emma rewrote her question. Why can you trust the second answer more?"
cards:
  - id: "A"
    label: "Before"
    prompt: "Can I move out early?"
    answer: |
      Yes, a tenant can usually end the lease early by giving the landlord one month's notice. The deposit is generally returned.
  - id: "B"
    label: "After"
    prompt: "I want to move out early. First, find and quote word for word every clause in the lease about early termination and the deposit. Then, based only on those quotes, answer: how much notice I need to give and whether I'll get my deposit back. If something isn't in the lease, say so."
    answer: |
      Quotes from the lease:
      1. Clause 7.2: “The Tenant may terminate this Agreement early by giving the Landlord not less than 60 calendar days' written notice.”
      2. Clause 4.3: “The deposit shall be returned to the Tenant provided there are no arrears and no damage to the property.”

      Answer: you need to give written notice at least 60 days in advance, not one month. You'll get the deposit back if there are no arrears and no damage. The lease doesn't say how soon the deposit must be returned.
options:
  - text: "Every conclusion rests on a word-for-word quote with a clause number, so it can be checked"
    correct: true
    why: "Yes. Emma can open clause 7.2 and compare. And it exposed a mistake in the first answer: her lease says 60 days, not one month."
  - text: "The second answer is longer, so it's more detailed and accurate"
    correct: false
    why: "Length doesn't guarantee accuracy. What earns trust is that every claim is backed by the text of the lease."
  - text: "Claude read the lease more carefully the second time because Emma asked more politely"
    correct: false
    why: "Politeness has nothing to do with it. The instructions did the work: quotes first, then the conclusion, based only on the lease."
  - text: "The answers are the same, only the layout is different"
    correct: false
    why: "No, they contradict each other: “one month” versus “60 days”. The mistake in the first answer could have cost Emma money."
hint: "Find something in answer B that Emma can check in the lease herself."
:::

::: step explain
The formula for a reliable question about a document:

1. **Quotes first:** “Find and quote word for word the clauses about…”
2. **Then the answer:** “Based only on those quotes, answer…”
3. **Permission to say “no”:** “If it isn't in the document, say so.”

The third part matters: Claude won't make things up just to please you.
:::

::: exercise find_error
id: "b3.3.e2"
type: "find_error"
question: "Daniel in Bristol is asking about his kitchen renovation contract. Tap every fragment that could lead Claude to answer “off the top of its head”."
prompt: "Look at the contract with the builder. What are the penalties for delays? If something isn't there, make up what's usual. Answer in your own words, no quotes."
segments:
  - text: "Look at the contract with the builder. "
    weak: false
    why: "It's clear which document this is about. A perfectly good start."
  - text: "What are the penalties for delays? "
    weak: false
    why: "A specific question: Claude knows what to look for in the contract."
  - text: "If something isn't there, make up what's usual. "
    weak: true
    why: "This is a direct invitation to invent things. Daniel won't be able to tell a clause from the contract apart from “what's usual”. Better: “If it isn't in the contract, say so.”"
  - text: "Answer in your own words, no quotes."
    weak: true
    why: "Without quotes, there's no way to check the answer. Better: “First quote the clauses about penalties word for word, then explain them in plain English.”"
hint: "Look for places where Claude is allowed to stray from the document or not show where the answer comes from."
:::

::: step explain
One more technique, for when you paste a long text straight into your message rather than attaching a file. Put *the text first and the question at the end*. That way Claude reads the material already knowing what you want, and the question doesn't get lost at the top.

Anthropic says that in its tests, putting the question at the end improved answer quality by up to 30%.
:::

::: exercise single_choice
id: "b3.3.e3"
type: "single_choice"
question: "Sarah is pasting a long letter from her property management company (3 pages of text) into the chat. What's the best way to structure her message?"
options:
  - text: "The full letter first, then the question at the end: “What are they asking me to do, and by when? Quote the relevant sentences first.”"
    correct: true
    why: "Material at the top, question at the bottom. That's what Anthropic recommends for long texts. Plus quotes as a bonus: the answer can be checked against the letter."
  - text: "The question first, then the three pages of the letter, and nothing else"
    correct: false
    why: "It'll work, but not as well. For long texts Anthropic recommends putting the question at the end, after the material."
  - text: "Half the question at the start, half at the end, with the letter in the middle"
    correct: false
    why: "The question is split, and Claude may only half understand it. Keep the whole question after the text."
  - text: "Send just the letter, then ask the question in another message a couple of minutes later"
    correct: false
    why: "An extra round: Claude will respond to the letter without a question, probably with a summary. Easier to add the question at the end straight away."
hint: "Remember the rule: “material at the top, question at the bottom”."
:::

  - text: "The task: “I want to find out whether I can get my deposit back”"

    why: "Quotes before conclusions. That way Claude focuses on the right passages and doesn't make things up."
  - text: "“Then, based on them, answer in 2–3 sentences”"

::: step explain
Now it's your turn. Write a prompt about a lease: Claude needs to find the termination terms and back them up with quotes.

Imagine any lease you like: a flat in Vilnius, a room in Lyon, an office in Porto. Don't copy personal details from the lease into your prompt: names and ID numbers aren't needed for the question.
:::

    Then, based only on those quotes, answer in a short list:
    1) how much notice I need to give and in what form;
    2) whether there's a penalty, and how much;

:::

::: step summary
**For documents: quotes first, then the answer, plus permission to say “it isn't there”.**

If you paste a long text into your message, put it at the top and the question at the bottom. An answer with quotes can be checked in a minute.
