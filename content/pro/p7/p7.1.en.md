::: step hook
Liam in Cork is building a support chatbot for a broadband provider. He's rewriting the system prompt for the sixth time. On the team call, an argument breaks out:

```prompt
— The new version is better!
— I think it's worse. Yesterday the bot was rude to a customer.
— But its answers are shorter now…
```

A week has gone by, and there's still no answer to “Is it better?” What is the team missing?
:::

::: step explain
They're missing *success criteria*: a written answer, agreed in advance, to the question “What does good look like?”

Anthropic's prompt engineering guide doesn't start with techniques. It starts with three things you need before them:

1. clear success criteria;
2. a way to test the prompt against those criteria in practice;
3. a first draft of the prompt.

Without the first two, “it's better” is just a feeling.
:::

::: step explain
A good criterion passes four checks:

- *specific*: not “does a good job” but “correctly identifies what the customer is asking about”;
- *measurable*: a number or a clear scale;
- *achievable*: based on past experiments, benchmarks or expert knowledge;
- *relevant*: it matters to your users.

Bad: “the bot answers questions about plans well”. Good: “on 200 questions about plans, the price matches the price list in 98% of answers”.
:::

::: exercise single_choice
id: "p7.1.e1"
type: "single_choice"
question: "Which success criterion for Liam's bot can be checked without an argument on the call?"
options:
  - text: "On 100 real messages complaining about slow speeds, the bot suggests restarting the router and checking the cable before handing over to an agent in 90% of cases"
    correct: true
    why: "There's a dataset (100 messages), an action that's easy to check and a threshold (90%). Anyone on the team can count the result and get the same number."
  - text: "The bot answers customers well and professionally"
    correct: false
    why: "That's a wish, not a criterion. Everyone reads “well” differently, so the argument on the call will carry on."
  - text: "The bot never makes mistakes"
    correct: false
    why: "Sounds strict, but it's not achievable: a language model always has some error rate. A realistic criterion names an acceptable percentage."
  - text: "The manager likes the new version of the prompt more than the old one"
    correct: false
    why: "One person's opinion can't be repeated on the next version of the prompt. They like it today, not tomorrow, and there's nothing to compare against."
hint: "Imagine two people checking the bot against this criterion independently. Which option gives them the same result?"
:::

::: exercise match_pairs
id: "p7.1.e2"
type: "match_pairs"
question: "Match each property of a good criterion with the example that shows it."
pairs:
  - left: "Specific"
    right: "“The bot correctly identifies the topic: billing, speed, installation or other”"
    why: "Instead of a vague “works well”, it names a specific result: which task the bot has to get right."
  - left: "Measurable"
    right: "“At least 95% of answers are under 80 words”"
    why: "There's a number and a threshold. Answer length can be counted by code across a thousand examples."
  - left: "Achievable"
    right: "“82% of answers are correct now; the target for this quarter is 88%”"
    why: "The target builds on the current result rather than a dream of 100%. That way the criterion shows real progress."
  - left: "Relevant"
    right: "“Prices in answers match the price list, because that's exactly what customers complain about”"
    why: "The criterion is tied to a real pain point for users. Measuring something nobody cares about is wasted effort."
hint: "Measurable is about a number, achievable is about a realistic starting point, relevant is about value for users."
:::

::: step explain
There are usually several criteria: accuracy, consistency, tone, privacy, use of context, latency and cost.

Not every failed criterion is fixed by the prompt. Anthropic's guide says it directly: latency and cost are sometimes easier to improve by choosing a different model. You edit the prompt where the problem is in the instructions.
:::

::: exercise multi_choice
id: "p7.1.e3"
type: "multi_choice"
question: "Liam's bot failed several criteria. Which problems does it make sense to tackle by editing the prompt first? Select all that apply."
options:
  - text: "The tone is too formal, and customers complain about corporate jargon"
    correct: true
    why: "Tone and style respond well to instructions: a role, a description of the tone, an example of a good answer."
  - text: "The bot quotes plans that aren't on the price list"
    correct: true
    why: "That's a job for the prompt and the data: put the price list in context, tell the bot to rely only on it and allow it to say “I don't know”."
  - text: "An answer takes 6 seconds to arrive, and it needs to be under 2"
    correct: false
    why: "Latency is often easier to improve by picking a faster model or changing settings, not by rewording the prompt. Anthropic's guide uses exactly this example."
  - text: "One conversation costs three times what the budget allows"
    correct: false
    why: "Cost is also often easier to cut with a different model, caching or batch processing. Rewriting the prompt isn't the main lever here."
  - text: "The bot replies with walls of text instead of 2–3 sentences"
    correct: true
    why: "Answer length and format are set in the prompt: “2–3 sentences, no lists”. That's a classic format instruction."
hint: "Think about what depends on the wording of the instructions and what depends on the model itself and the infrastructure."
:::

::: exercise find_error
id: "p7.1.e4"
type: "find_error"
question: "Liam drafted success criteria for the next version of the bot. Tap every statement that can't be checked objectively."
prompt: "The bot answers politely and pleasantly. On 200 questions about plans, the price matches the price list in at least 98% of answers. The bot answers quickly. The bot never gets installation addresses wrong."
segments:
  - text: "The bot answers politely and pleasantly. "
    weak: true
    why: "No scale and no threshold. Better: “on a tone scale from 1 to 5, the average score is at least 4 across 100 conversations”, and describe what 1 and 5 mean."
  - text: "On 200 questions about plans, the price matches the price list in at least 98% of answers. "
    weak: false
    why: "A model criterion: there's a dataset, a way to check it and a threshold."
  - text: "The bot answers quickly. "
    weak: true
    why: "How quick is “quickly”? Better: “95% of answers arrive in under 2 seconds”."
  - text: "The bot never gets installation addresses wrong."
    weak: true
    why: "Not achievable: “never” doesn't happen. Name an acceptable error rate and what to do if the address isn't found, for example hand over to an agent."
hint: "Look for words without a number or threshold, and for promises like “always” and “never”."
:::

::: step explain
Criteria aren't only for testing. It's worth writing them into the prompt itself: Claude does better when it knows what a good answer looks like and why.

Now it's your turn: turn the bot's vague system prompt into one with explicit criteria.
:::

::: exercise improve_prompt
id: "p7.1.e5"
type: "improve_prompt"
question: "Improve the system prompt for a broadband provider's support bot. Spell out what counts as a good answer: length, tone, source of data and what to do if there's no answer."
weak_prompt: "You're a support bot for a broadband provider. Answer customers well and help them."
placeholder: "You're a support assistant for a broadband provider…"
ideas:
  - "Who the customers are and where they write"
  - "Answer length in words or sentences"
  - "Plans and prices only from the price list"
  - "When to hand the question to an agent"
  - "The price list in a separate tag"
min_chars: 120
hint: "Answer “What does good look like?” in the prompt: how long the answer is, what tone it uses, where prices come from and what to do if the data has no answer. Separate the data from the instructions with a tag."
review:
  task: "Lesson on success criteria (Professional level). The learner improves the system prompt of a broadband provider's support bot: “You're a support bot for a broadband provider. Answer customers well and help them.” A strong prompt has: a clear role and task (R1); context — who the customers are, where they write, why the constraints matter (R2); format — length, tone, shape of the answer (R3); the core of the lesson — explicit, checkable criteria for a good answer: for example a length limit, prices only from the price list, what to do when data is missing, when to hand over to an agent (R4); the price list or knowledge base separated from the instructions with tags (R7). Don't require real prices: the learner can leave a slot for the data or make them up. Words like “well” or “high quality” without explanation are the main gap."
  criteria: ["R1", "R2", "R3", "R4", "R7"]
  reference_prompt: |
    You're a support assistant for Net-Plus, a broadband provider in Cork. Customers write in the chat on our website, usually from their phones, and something has usually already stopped working, so they need a concrete next step quickly.

    A good answer:
    - 2–4 sentences, no more than 80 words;
    - a calm, friendly, polite tone, no corporate jargon;
    - prices and plan terms only from <tariffs>; if what's needed isn't there, say you'll check and offer to put the customer in touch with an agent;
    - for complaints about speed, first suggest restarting the router and checking the cable, and only if that doesn't help, hand over to an agent;
    - hand questions about paying off arrears or disconnection straight to an agent.

    <tariffs>
    [insert the current price list]
    </tariffs>
  show_reference: true
  fallback_feedback:
    - "Role: is it clear what job the bot does and for which company?"
    - "Context: does it say who the customers are, where they write and why your rules matter?"
    - "Criteria: could someone use the prompt to check whether an answer is good: length, tone, source of prices?"
    - "Knowledge boundary: does it say what to do if the answer isn't in the data?"
    - "Structure: are the price list or knowledge base separated from the instructions with tags?"
    - "Are there any words like “well” or “high quality” without explanation?"
:::

::: step summary
**First decide what “good” means, then write the prompt.**

A success criterion is specific, measurable, achievable and matters to users. Latency and cost are often easier to improve by choosing a model than by editing the prompt.
