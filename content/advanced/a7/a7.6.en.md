::: step hook
Mark from Dublin is organising an away day for a team of 15:

```prompt
Put together a plan for a team-building away day.
```

```claude
Plan: 9:00 — meet and coach transfer, 10:00 — team escape challenge, 13:00 — lunch, 15:00 — high ropes course, 18:00 — dinner by the campfire…
```

But half of Mark's team works remotely from other cities, the budget is tight, and one colleague is on crutches. Claude didn't know any of this. And Mark hadn't thought about half of it in advance himself.
:::

::: step explain
With a big task, you often don't know which context matters. The colleague test doesn't help: you can't see your own gaps.

The answer is an *interview*. Give a short task and ask Claude to question you first. The official Claude Code guide recommends starting big tasks this way: Claude will ask about things you haven't thought of yet. We're carrying this technique over to ordinary chat.
:::

::: step explain
A sample based on the guide:

```prompt
I want to organise an away day for a team of 15. Interview me in detail before suggesting anything. Don't ask obvious questions — dig into the hard parts I might not have considered. Keep going until you've covered everything important, then put together the final brief in one message.
```

Three key parts: *not the obvious*, *all the way through*, *a final brief*.
:::

::: step explain
What makes a good brief? The guide describes it for programmers, but the rule is universal. A good brief is *self-contained*: someone who didn't see the interview can understand it. It says what's *out of scope* and *how to check* that the result worked.
:::

    correct: true
    why: "The brief is self-contained: in a new chat Claude can't see the interview, so everything it needs must be in the text itself."
  - text: "What's out of scope: for example, “the office manager is arranging transport”"
    correct: true
    why: "Clear boundaries save time: Claude won't plan things that have already been sorted without it."
  - text: "How to tell the plan worked: “all 15 can take part, we stay within budget”"
    correct: true
    why: "These are the success criteria. Mark will check the finished plan against them, as in the last lesson."
  - text: "The full interview transcript, word for word"
    correct: false
    why: "A brief is a summary, not a transcript. The full conversation in a new chat would clutter up the context you were trying to leave behind."
hint: "Imagine the brief is read by someone who didn't see the interview. What do they need, and what's surplus?"
:::

::: step summary
**Big task? Let Claude interview you first.**

A short start, hard questions all the way through, a final brief in one message, and the work done in a new chat. For a task you can describe in one sentence, you don't need an interview.
