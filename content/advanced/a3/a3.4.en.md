::: step hook
Sam from Bristol is thinking about buying an electric car and starts some research:

```prompt
Tell me everything about electric cars.
```

A few minutes later: a long report on the history of Tesla, batteries and the environment. Impressive, but it doesn't answer his question. And what is Sam's question? Claude never found out.
:::

::: step explain
Research is expensive work: lots of searches, minutes of waiting, a noticeable chunk of your limit. So it's worth building the prompt from four parts:

1. **Question and purpose**: what decision you'll make.
2. **Boundaries**: region, time period, budget, what to leave out.
3. **Sources**: which ones to trust first.
4. **Report format**: table, takeaways, length.
:::

::: exercise match_pairs
id: "a3.4.e1"
type: "match_pairs"
question: "Match each part of the prompt with an example from Sam's research."
pairs:
  - left: "Question and purpose"
    right: "“I'm deciding whether to get an electric car instead of a petrol one: I drive about 40 miles a day around town”"
    why: "Now Claude isn't searching for “everything about electric cars” but for an answer to a specific decision with a specific mileage."
  - left: "Boundaries"
    right: "“UK only, budget up to £25,000, the market over the last year”"
    why: "Boundaries cut out the noise: US prices, luxury models and outdated data."
  - left: "Sources"
    right: "“Prices from dealer and listings websites, energy use from independent tests, not from ads”"
    why: "You say up front which sources to trust. A manufacturer's ad and an independent test are very different things."
  - left: "Report format"
    right: "“A table of 5 models and a half-page verdict: buy or not”"
    why: "It's clear when the job is done and what it should look like. Claude won't write a chapter on the history of Tesla."
hint: "Question: “what am I deciding and why”; boundaries: “where and within what limits”; sources: “who to trust”; format: “how to present it”."
:::

::: step explain
One more technique: ask Claude to *cross-check sources against each other*. A fact should count as reliable only if at least two independent sources confirm it. If they disagree, Claude should show both versions rather than quietly pick one.

For complex topics, you can also ask it to mark its confidence in each conclusion: high, medium or low.
:::

::: exercise build_prompt
id: "a3.4.e2"
type: "build_prompt"
question: "Build Sam's research prompt from the blocks. Leave out any blocks you don't need."
blocks:
  - id: "b1"
    text: "Help me decide whether to get an electric car instead of a petrol one: I drive about 40 miles a day around Bristol and can charge at home."
    role: "goal"
    why: "A question with a purpose and key details: daily mileage and home charging change the whole calculation."
  - id: "b2"
    text: "Consider models sold in the UK, up to £25,000, using data from the last year."
    role: "context"
    why: "Boundaries: region, budget and time period. Without them, other markets and outdated prices will end up in the report."
  - id: "b3"
    text: "Take prices from dealer and listings websites, and range from independent tests. Treat a fact as reliable if two sources confirm it; if they disagree, show both versions."
    role: "context"
    why: "Source requirements and cross-checking between them. That way, unchecked marketing figures won't end up in the report."
  - id: "b4"
    text: "Report: a table of 5 models (price, real-world range, cost per 100 miles), with a half-page conclusion below it. A link for every figure, and if you can't find something, say so."
    role: "format"
    why: "Format and criteria for done: how many models, which columns, how long the conclusion is, links and honesty about gaps."
  - id: "b5"
    text: "Tell me everything you know about electric cars."
    role: "noise"
    why: "This is the very prompt from the start of the lesson. “Everything” with no purpose gets you a long report with no answer."
  - id: "b6"
    text: "My phone number and address, in case they're useful."
    role: "noise"
    why: "Market research doesn't need personal data, and it shouldn't end up in the chat."
target_order: ["b1", "b2", "b3", "b4"]
hint: "Order: question and purpose, boundaries, sources, format. Vague and personal stuff goes to one side."
:::

::: exercise single_choice
id: "a3.4.e3"
type: "single_choice"
question: "Why ask Claude to cross-check sources against each other?"
options:
  - text: "So one website's mistake doesn't become a “fact” in the report, and you can see where sources disagree"
    correct: true
    why: "A single source can be wrong or out of date. Cross-checking shows what you can rely on and where there's doubt."
  - text: "So the report comes out longer and more impressive"
    correct: false
    why: "Length has nothing to do with it. The goal is reliable conclusions, not volume."
  - text: "So Claude doesn't waste time searching"
    correct: false
    why: "Quite the opposite: cross-checking takes more searches. That's the price of reliability."
  - text: "So Claude picks the most popular version and doesn't bother you with disagreements"
    correct: false
    why: "You need to see disagreements, not hide them. The popular version isn't necessarily the right one."
hint: "Think about what happens if the whole conclusion rests on a single page."
:::

::: exercise compare_answers
id: "a3.4.e4"
type: "compare_answers"
variant: "which_better"
question: "Sam got two excerpts from reports on the range of the same model. Which one is more useful for his decision?"
cards:
  - id: "A"
    label: "Excerpt A"
    prompt: "Find out the real-world range of this model."
    answer: |
      Range: 280 miles. A great choice for town and longer trips.
  - id: "B"
    label: "Excerpt B"
    prompt: "Find out the real-world range of this model. Cross-check sources against each other, and show disagreements and your confidence."
    answer: |
      The manufacturer claims 280 miles [1]. Two independent winter tests showed 185–200 miles [2][3]. Confidence: medium — there are few tests, and I couldn't find summer data.
options:
  - text: "Excerpt B"
    correct: true
    why: "You can see where each figure comes from, where the sources disagree and how confident Claude is. Sam can decide for himself whether 185 miles in winter is enough."
  - text: "Excerpt A"
    correct: false
    why: "It sounds confident, but it's a marketing figure with no link and no cross-checking. Sam could be in for a nasty surprise in winter."
hint: "In which excerpt can you see where the figures come from and where sources disagree?"
:::

::: step explain
Now it's your turn. Pick a task of your own where you need to get to grips with a topic before deciding: a move, a purchase, choosing a course, a supplier or a school. Improve the weak prompt so it has all four parts plus cross-checking of sources.
:::

::: exercise improve_prompt
id: "a3.4.e5"
type: "improve_prompt"
question: "Improve the research prompt: add a question with a purpose, boundaries, source requirements and cross-checking, and a report format. You can swap the topic for your own."
weak_prompt: "Find information about moving to Portugal"
placeholder: "Help me decide…"
ideas:
  - "What decision you'll make and why"
  - "City, timing, budget, who's in the family"
  - "Which sources to trust"
  - "Two sources per fact, show disagreements"
  - "Table and conclusion, links for figures"
  - "If nothing's found, say so"
min_chars: 100
hint: "Go through the four parts: what you're deciding and why, within what limits (where, when, how much), which sources to trust and how to cross-check them, and what the report should look like."
review:
  task: "Lesson on research prompts (web search or Research). The learner improves the weak prompt “Find information about moving to Portugal” or writes their own topic. A strong prompt: a question with a purpose — what decision the learner will make (R1); boundaries and context — region, period, budget, situation (R2); report format — table, takeaways, length (R3); success criteria — when the job is done, how many options, what to leave out (R4); reliance on sources — which to trust, cross-checking between sources, disagreements shown, “not found” is acceptable, links for facts (R8). Bonus: asking for confidence levels on conclusions. Don't require personal data; if it's there (document numbers, addresses, other people's names), R10 applies."
  criteria: ["R1", "R2", "R3", "R4", "R8"]
  reference_prompt: |
    Help me decide whether our family (two adults and a 7-year-old) should move from Manchester to Lisbon or Porto next spring. I'm a software developer working remotely; my wife is an accountant and will look for work locally.
    Compare the two cities on rent for a 2-bedroom flat, the cost of private and state primary schools, and demand for English-speaking accountants.
    Use data from the last year. Take rents from listings websites, schools from their own websites, and vacancies from job sites. Treat a fact as reliable if two sources confirm it; if they disagree, show both figures.
    Report: a “parameter — Lisbon — Porto” table with a link for every figure, and a half-page conclusion below it with a confidence level. If you can't find something, say so.
  show_reference: true
  fallback_feedback:
    - "Question: is it clear what decision you'll make and why?"
    - "Boundaries: did you give the region, timing, budget and situation?"
    - "Sources: did you say which sources to trust?"
    - "Cross-checking: are you asking for facts confirmed by two sources and disagreements shown?"
    - "Format: did you set the shape of the report — table, conclusion, links?"
    - "Honesty: did you allow Claude to say “not found”?"
:::

::: step summary
**A strong research prompt: question and purpose, boundaries, sources, format.**

Ask Claude to cross-check sources against each other and show disagreements. An honest “couldn't find it” beats a confident fabrication.
:::
