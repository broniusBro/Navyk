::: step hook
Lukas in Antwerp sells board games online. He exported a year of sales to a CSV — 4,000 rows — and writes:

```prompt
Analyse my sales
```

```claude
I've analysed the file. 4,012 orders in total, revenue of €168,500, average order value €42. The most popular category is family games…
```

The numbers are correct, but Lukas still has no idea what to do. Why?
:::

::: step explain
Because “analyse” isn't a question. Data can answer a hundred questions, and Claude picked some of its own.

A strong analysis starts with a *question for the data*: what are you trying to decide? For example: “Which months should I stock up for?” or “Which games have stopped selling?”
:::

::: exercise single_choice
id: "a2.3.e1"
type: "single_choice"
question: "Lukas is deciding how many games to order for Christmas. Which prompt for the CSV is best?"
options:
  - text: "“Work out monthly revenue for the last 2 years and show how sales grew in November and December by category. I need to decide how much stock to order for Christmas”"
    correct: true
    why: "It has a question, the right slice of the data (months, categories), and the why. Claude will calculate exactly what helps with the order."
  - text: "“Analyse my sales in as much detail as possible”"
    correct: false
    why: "“Detail” doesn't replace a question. You'll get lots of numbers but no answer about the Christmas order."
  - text: "“How many games should I order for Christmas?” with no file"
    correct: false
    why: "Without data, Claude will answer in general terms or start guessing. The answer has to be based on Lukas's sales."
  - text: "“Make a nice chart”"
    correct: false
    why: "A chart of what? What goes on the time axis, what's the height? A pretty chart won't help if it doesn't answer a question."
hint: "Look for the prompt that has a question, the data and the reason it's needed."
:::

::: step explain
What else to tell Claude about the data:

- **what's in the columns**, if the names aren't obvious (“qty” — units, “sum” — euros);
- **which period** matters;
- **what to do with messy data**: empty cells, duplicates, returns;
- **which chart** and what to highlight on it.

Claude draws the chart with code and gives it to you as a PNG image.
:::

::: exercise match_pairs
id: "a2.3.e2"
type: "match_pairs"
question: "Match each question about the data with the chart type that answers it best."
pairs:
  - left: "How has monthly revenue changed over 2 years?"
    right: "Line chart: time from left to right"
    why: "A line shows change over time well: growth, dips, seasonality."
  - left: "Which game category brings in the most money?"
    right: "Bar chart sorted from highest to lowest"
    why: "Bars are easy to compare with each other, and sorting shows the leader at once."
  - left: "Is a game's price related to how many copies sell?"
    right: "Scatter plot: price on one axis, sales on the other"
    why: "Each game is a dot. If the dots line up, there's a relationship; if they're scattered, there isn't."
  - left: "Which order value is most common: small, medium or large?"
    right: "Histogram: how many orders fall into each price range"
    why: "A histogram shows the distribution: where the “hump” is and whether there are rare large orders."
hint: "Time → line, comparison → bars, link between two numbers → dots, “how often does it happen” → histogram."
:::

::: exercise find_error
id: "a2.3.e3"
type: "find_error"
question: "Sophie in Lille uploaded a CSV of job applications. Tap the weak spots in her prompt."
prompt: "Count which sources bring in the most applications, and draw a chart. Just delete empty rows and duplicates, don't tell me. If anything's unclear, make it up."
segments:
  - text: "Count which sources bring in the most applications, "
    weak: false
    why: "There's a clear question for the data. She could add why (for example, where to spend the advertising budget), but this part is good."
  - text: "and draw a chart. "
    weak: false
    why: "It'll do, but it's a bit thin: better to name the chart type and what to highlight. We won't count it as a weak spot — the real gaps are next."
  - text: "Just delete empty rows and duplicates, don't tell me. "
    weak: true
    why: "Cleaning data is fine; doing it silently isn't. If a third of the rows are duplicates, that changes the conclusions. Better: “remove duplicates and tell me how many rows you removed”."
  - text: "If anything's unclear, make it up."
    weak: true
    why: "This is a direct invitation to invent things. Better: “if you can't tell the source, put those rows in a separate ‘unknown’ group”."
hint: "Look for places where Claude will hide something important about the data from you, or start guessing."
:::

::: step explain
Got a chart? Don't rush to forward it to your boss.

Ask to see the *numbers behind the chart*: the table it was drawn from. Check one or two figures against the original file. And ask how many rows Claude threw out while cleaning.

A great-looking chart with wrong data is more dangerous than no chart at all.
:::

::: exercise multi_choice
id: "a2.3.e4"
type: "multi_choice"
question: "Claude has sent an analysis and chart of Lukas's sales. What's sensible to do before deciding on the order? Select all that apply."
options:
  - text: "Ask for the table of numbers the chart was built from"
    correct: true
    why: "That way you see exact values, not “roughly the height of the bar”, and can check them."
  - text: "Check one month's total against the original file or the shop's report"
    correct: true
    why: "A single spot check quickly shows whether Claude read the data correctly."
  - text: "Ask how many rows were removed as duplicates or empty"
    correct: true
    why: "Cleaning data changes the result. You need to know what was removed and how much."
  - text: "Accept the conclusion as is: Claude calculated with code, so there can't be mistakes"
    correct: false
    why: "Code calculates precisely, but Claude may have misread a column, for example treating returns as sales. You still need to check."
  - text: "Ask Claude to “make the conclusions more confident”"
    correct: false
    why: "Confidence doesn't make conclusions more accurate. Quite the opposite: it's useful when Claude is honest about where the data falls short."
hint: "Think about what can go wrong between the original file and the final image."
:::

::: exercise write_prompt
id: "a2.3.e5"
type: "write_prompt"
question: "Write a prompt for your CSV (imagine the file is attached): the question for the data and why, what's in the columns, what to do with messy data, which chart, and how to check the result."
placeholder: "The file contains… I want to understand…"
ideas:
  - "What are you trying to decide?"
  - "What's in the columns?"
  - "Which period?"
  - "What to do with empty rows and duplicates?"
  - "Which chart, and what to highlight?"
  - "Ask for the table behind the chart"
min_chars: 100
hint: "Start with the question and why you need the answer. Then describe the columns, say what to do with empty rows and duplicates, name the chart type and ask for the table of numbers behind it."
review:
  task: "The learner writes a prompt to analyse an attached CSV (the file is implied) with a PNG chart. The lesson teaches: start with a specific question for the data and a goal (R1); explain the columns, period and source (R2); describe the output — chart type, what to highlight, labels, the table of numbers behind the chart (R3); don't hide data cleaning and don't guess at unclear entries — report how many rows were removed and put unclear entries in a separate group (R4, R8). Analysis steps in order are a plus (R5). If the learner included card numbers, account numbers or other people's personal data — R10."
  criteria: ["R1", "R2", "R3", "R4", "R5", "R8"]
  reference_prompt: |
    The attached CSV has my spending from my bank statement for 2026: columns date, amount (in euros, minus means spending), description (transaction description).
    I want to understand which categories take the most money and in which months I spend more, so that from next year I can save €200 a month.
    1. Sort the spending into categories: groceries, eating out, transport, subscriptions, clothes, other. Put transactions you can't categorise into an “unclear” group; don't guess.
    2. Don't count transfers between my own accounts as spending. Tell me how many of those rows you removed.
    3. Make a PNG bar chart: spending by category, highest to lowest, with the amounts labelled. And a line chart of spending by month, with the most expensive month highlighted.
    4. Under the charts, give the table of numbers and 3 ideas for where I could save.
  show_reference: true
  fallback_feedback:
    - "Is there a question for the data, and why do you need the answer?"
    - "Have you explained what's in the columns and what period the data covers?"
    - "Have you said what to do with empty rows, duplicates and unclear entries — and to report it?"
    - "Have you named the chart type and what to highlight on it?"
    - "Did you ask for the table of numbers behind the chart so you can check it?"
    - "Is your prompt free of card numbers, account numbers and other people's personal data?"
:::

::: step summary
**Question first, chart second.** Say what you want to decide, what's in the columns and what to do with messy data. Pick the chart to fit the question.

Before deciding anything, ask for the numbers behind the chart and check one or two against the original.

:::
